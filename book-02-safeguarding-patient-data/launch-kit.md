### Post 2: The Data Lifecycle (Launch Day)

Every piece of patient data goes through five phases:

Collect → Store → Use → Share → Delete

In traditional healthcare IT, this is straightforward. In AI, it breaks.

Because AI does not just "access" data. It ingests it. Copies it. Transforms it. Stores it in new locations. Embeds it in model weights that cannot be deleted.

My new book, **Safeguarding Confidential Patient Data in the Age of AI**, maps every phase of this lifecycle — and where each one fails in AI projects.

The most dangerous moment? When someone says, "We deleted the data."

Ask them:
1. Did you delete it from all backup systems?
2. Did you delete it from all vendor systems?
3. Did you delete it from log files?
4. Did you delete it from the trained model's weights?
5. How did you verify deletion?

If the answer to any of these is "I don't know," the data is not deleted.

### Post 3: The Consent Gap

India's DPDP Act 2023 changed the consent landscape.

If a hospital wants to use patient data for AI training, the consent form must explicitly say so.

"I consent to the use of my data for treatment purposes" is not enough.

The consent must specifically cover:
- What data will be used
- For what AI purpose
- Who will have access
- How long the data will be retained
- Whether the data will be shared with third parties
- Whether the data will be transferred outside India

Most hospitals do not have consent forms that cover AI use.

My new book explains this in plain language — with templates and checklists you can use today.

## YouTube Scripts

### Video 1: "Can You Train AI on Patient Data?"

**Duration:** 5-7 minutes

**Script:**

[INTRO]
Can you train an AI model on patient data? The short answer is: maybe. The long answer depends on the legal framework, the consent obtained, and the data handling practices.

[SECTION 1: THE LEGAL LANDSCAPE]
India's DPDP Act says health data is sensitive personal data. You need explicit consent to process it — including for AI training.

HIPAA says PHI can be used for treatment, payment, and healthcare operations without additional authorization. But AI training is not automatically "healthcare operations." You need to verify.

GDPR says health data is a special category. Processing requires explicit consent or a specific legal basis. Scientific research has provisions, but AI training is not automatically "research."

[SECTION 2: THE FOUR OPTIONS]
Option 1: Obtain explicit consent. The safest approach across all frameworks. But most existing consent forms do not cover AI use.

Option 2: De-identify the data. If the data is properly de-identified, it is no longer personal data. But de-identification is not always reliable, and many AI models need data elements that are identifiers.

Option 3: Use synthetic data. Generate artificial data that preserves statistical properties. No consent required. But quality depends on the generation method.

Option 4: Use federated learning. Train the model without centralizing the data. Data never leaves the hospital. But more complex to implement.

[SECTION 3: THE DECISION FRAMEWORK]
Here is the simple decision framework:

Is the data de-identified? If yes, you can train.

Do you have explicit consent? If yes, you can train.

Does a legal exception apply? If yes, you can train — but verify.

Can you use synthetic data or federated learning? Consider these alternatives.

If none of these apply, you cannot train on patient data.

### Video 2: "The Vendor Problem in Healthcare AI"

**Duration:** 5-7 minutes

**Script:**

[INTRO]
Your AI vendor stores your patient data. But do you know where? Do you know who can access it? Do you know what happens when the contract ends?

[SECTION 1: THE RISK]
Most hospital teams cannot answer these questions. And that is the problem.

The vendor stores data in a cloud. Which cloud? Which region? Is it encrypted? Who holds the keys?

The vendor has employees. Who are they? What background checks were performed? Can they access patient data?

The vendor uses sub-processors. Who are they? Where are they located? What data can they see?

[SECTION 2: THE CONTRACT]
Every AI vendor contract should include three things:

A Data Processing Agreement — specifying what the vendor can and cannot do with the data.

A Business Associate Agreement — required under HIPAA for any vendor that processes PHI.

AI-specific clauses — covering model training restrictions, data copying restrictions, model deletion, audit rights, and cross-border transfer restrictions.

[SECTION 3: THE CHECKLIST]
Before signing a contract with an AI vendor:

Request and review SOC 2 Type II report.
Verify ISO 27001 certification.
Identify all sub-processors.
Confirm cloud provider and region.
Verify encryption at rest and in transit.
Confirm breach notification timeline.
Confirm data return and deletion process.

### Video 3: "What Is the DPDP Act and Why Does It Matter for Healthcare AI?"

**Duration:** 5-7 minutes

**Script:**

[INTRO]
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 is now law. If you work in healthcare AI in India, this matters.

[SECTION 1: THE BASICS]
The DPDP Act defines personal data as any data about a natural person who is identifiable.

Sensitive personal data includes health data, financial data, biometric data, genetic data, and more.

If your hospital processes patient data, you are a "data fiduciary" under the Act. You have legal obligations.

[SECTION 2: CONSENT]
The DPDP Act requires consent to be:
- Free — not bundled with other terms
- Specific — clearly stating what data will be collected and why
- Informed — the patient must understand what they are consenting to
- Unambiguous — clear affirmative action
- Revocable — the patient can withdraw consent at any time

For AI projects, the consent form must explicitly cover AI use. "I consent to treatment" is not enough.

[SECTION 3: PENALTIES]
Non-compliance can result in penalties up to INR 250 crore. The Data Protection Board of India is operational and actively investigating complaints.

[SECTION 4: WHAT TO DO]
Map every data element you plan to use. Determine the legal basis. Update consent forms. Implement security safeguards. Document everything.
